One morning, as Gregor Samsa was waking up from anxious dreams, he discovered that in his bed he had been changed into a monstrous verminous bug. He lay on his armor-hard back and saw, as he lifted his head up a little, his brown, arched abdomen divided up into rigid bow-like sections. His blanket perched high on this belly could hardly stay in place; it seemed about to slide off completely. His numerous legs, pitifully thin in comparison to the rest of his circumference, flickered helplessly before his eyes.

“What’s happened to me?” he thought. It was no dream. His room, a proper room for a human being, only somewhat too small, lay quietly between the four well-known. walls. Above the table, on which an unpacked collection of sample cloth goods was spread out—Samsa was a traveling salesman—hung the picture which he had cut out of an illustrated magazine a little while ago and set in a pretty gilt frame. It was a picture of a woman with a fur hat and a fur boa. She sat erect, lifting up in the direction of the viewer a solid fur muff into which her entire forearm had disappeared.

## The same reflection rendered as fluid prose

<<<TRANSLATION
A room for a human being, and Gregor still thinking inside it. That catches me more than the reassurance that this is no dream. The room has not become strange in the way his body has: there are the familiar walls, the table, the things he has put there. Maybe that familiarity gives him something to hold on to. Or maybe a room still made to a human measure makes his changed body feel more out of place. I cannot yet tell which weight those walls carry for him.

Earlier I was surprised that the enormous shock narrowed to a blanket that would not stay on. That little domestic failure made the impossible oddly tangible, even almost funny, though his helpless legs stopped it becoming an easy joke. Now that same familiarity has spread into the whole room. It still does not tell me whether the ordinary things help him or sharpen the mismatch.

The picture tempts me with something more personal. He cut it from a magazine and chose a pretty gilt frame for it; here is something he actually selected. I want that choice to bring me closer to him. Maybe the fur-clad woman offers glamour, maybe comfort, maybe the picture is simply decoration. I do not know why he liked it. I had cautioned myself against turning helplessness into a story about his values: the wish for control might have been mine, rather than his. Now I have a past choice to look at, but its reason is still missing. Caring about his experience means leaving him more private life than I can infer from one framed image.

And the picture hangs above cloth samples. I want to imagine a private refuge, yet his work shares the room with his decoration. Perhaps the samples are simply there because he is a traveling salesman. Perhaps work occupies more of his personal space than he would choose. I do not yet know whether he feels at home in that role, and familiar walls alone cannot establish comfort. The possible refuge and the possible intrusion sit together in the same small room.

Nor can those walls answer his question about what has happened. 'It was no dream' closes one easy way out, but does not tell me why the transformation happened or what he feels about it. I can take this waking predicament seriously without pretending to understand its cause. For now I know a little more of his surroundings, and still have to leave open what home feels like from inside this body.
