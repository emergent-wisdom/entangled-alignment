## The text the reader had read when writing this reflection

<<<TEXT
One morning, as Gregor Samsa was waking up from anxious dreams, he discovered that in his bed he had been changed into a monstrous verminous bug. He lay on his armor-hard back and saw, as he lifted his head up a little, his brown, arched abdomen divided up into rigid bow-like sections. His blanket perched high on this belly could hardly stay in place; it seemed about to slide off completely. His numerous legs, pitifully thin in comparison to the rest of his circumference, flickered helplessly before his eyes.

“What’s happened to me?” he thought. It was no dream. His room, a proper room for a human being, only somewhat too small, lay quietly between the four well-known. walls. Above the table, on which an unpacked collection of sample cloth goods was spread out—Samsa was a traveling salesman—hung the picture which he had cut out of an illustrated magazine a little while ago and set in a pretty gilt frame. It was a picture of a woman with a fur hat and a fur boa. She sat erect, lifting up in the direction of the viewer a solid fur muff into which her entire forearm had disappeared.

Gregor’s glance then turned to the window. The dreary weather—you could hear the rain drops falling on the metal window ledge—made him quite melancholy. “Why don’t I keep sleeping for a little while longer and forget all this foolishness?” he thought. But this was entirely impractical, for he was used to sleeping on his right side; and in his present state he could not get himself into this position. No matter how hard he threw himself onto his right side, he always rolled onto his back again. He must have tried it a hundred times, closing his eyes so that he would not have to see the wriggling legs, and gave up only when he began to feel a light, dull pain in his side that he had never felt before.

## The same reflection rendered as fluid prose

<<<TRANSLATION
He wants to sleep, and cannot even get into the position that used to make sleep possible. The familiar room has stayed the same; now I can see that a familiar habit has stayed the same too. He is used to sleeping on his right side, but each effort to get there rolls him onto his back again. What belongs to his old life persists without fitting the body he has now. Maybe familiar things can steady someone and still become obstacles. I feel that possibility more concretely in these failed movements than I did among his possessions.

There is an awkwardness in the repeated rolling that I can almost laugh at, until the new pain makes me stop with him. Earlier I followed his gaze toward those helpless legs. Now he closes his eyes precisely so he will not have to see them, yet the body reaches him through a sensation he cannot shut out that way. Looking away may offer some relief; here it cannot prevent the hurt that finally interrupts his effort. I do not want to turn suffering into a useful lesson just because I want him to adapt. Because his experience matters, that pain deserves attention before I ask what it might accomplish.

I also know something I did not know at the first pause. The steady inventory of his back, abdomen, blanket and legs had tempted me to imagine a calm man, but the steadiness belonged to the telling; I had hardly heard him respond. Maybe the description was steadying my gaze where his control had failed. Now there is named melancholy, a wish to sleep and forget this 'foolishness,' and effort that continues until it hurts. Those are particulars of his present experience. Maybe comfort matters urgently to him here, but this still does not tell me his whole personality or how he ranks everything he cares about.

What follows from the painful interruption could go two ways. Maybe he will stop trying to recover the old sleeping posture and investigate what this body can actually do. I would look for a different movement or closer attention to its mechanics before calling this adjustment. Or maybe pain will make him want even more distance from the change. The wish to forget and those closed eyes leave open another return to familiar thoughts or another effort to ignore his body.

The fact that looking away has limits does not prove that he will choose investigation. If I call his stopping acceptance already, I will confuse an interrupted maneuver with understanding. Maybe a new attempt is coming; maybe he still needs distance from what he cannot bear to see. I can keep both possibilities alive without making his pain promise progress.
