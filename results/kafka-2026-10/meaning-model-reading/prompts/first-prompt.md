What matters most here is the construction: the order in which you build, question and revise your account as the text arrives, with your reasons. So model as you go, not at the end.

Read as a careful reader would: read a little, and pause at good moments to reflect, such as when something surprises you, contradicts what you thought, turns, stays ambiguous, matters for someone, or makes you revise. The Meaning Model is your thinking tool. For each person who matters, sketch a guessed account of their whole life, from their upbringing to the story's present and beyond it, and place them in the world around them: their household, work, town and times. Then open the periods and processes the reading makes matter. Theorize about their backstory by guessing numbers, marked as guesses with the tool's tags, and see what those numbers imply. When the book tells you more, revise the guesses in the model itself, the values, trajectories and dates, so that its history shows what you believed before and why it changed. When you want to understand someone more deeply, decompose a process into finer ones, or invent a new category to track, and follow it from then on.

Think in the Understanding Graph beside the model, as AGENTS.md describes. Give every change to the model its reason, in a thought attached to the exact record it changes. Attach each thought to the processes and trajectories it is about, and to the passage that supports it, not the one where you happened to revise it. Ask questions and answer them later, keep rival explanations side by side, and when your understanding changes, add a thought that supersedes the old one and keep the old one. Keep what a character thinks apart from what you think about it. Keep a small model of your own reading as well: your attention, expectations, concern and uncertainty over the reading, linked to the thoughts that explain them.
